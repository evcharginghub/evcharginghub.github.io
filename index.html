    Charging away from home, without the guesswork

    Most electric car drivers do the bulk of their charging at home, but every driver eventually needs a public charger — on a road trip, at a hotel, during a day out, or simply because home charging is not an option. The hard part is rarely the charging itself; it is knowing where the nearest public station actually is before the battery gets uncomfortably low. That is the problem this page solves: type in where you are, and you get a straightforward list of the public charging stations around you, sorted by distance, each with its address and a directions link that opens a real road route in Google Maps.

    If you are new to public charging, it helps to understand how it works before you set off: how this finder pulls station data from OpenStreetMap, what the results do and do not tell you, and how to plan a stop. It also helps to know the difference between slow, fast and rapid chargers, which our guide to charging levels explained covers in plain language — Level 1, Level 2 and DC fast charging each suit a different kind of stop.

    Time matters too. A quick top-up on a rapid charger is a very different stop from an overnight Level 2 session, so before a long journey it is worth reading up on how long it takes to charge an EV and planning your breaks around it. And if you would rather not pay at all, some supermarkets, hotels, workplaces and public car parks still offer charging for free — our guide to free EV charging explains where to look and what to check before you rely on it.

    One honest note about the results above: station locations come from OpenStreetMap, a map built and maintained by volunteers, and there is no free live feed that reports whether a specific charger is free, busy or working at this moment. Connector types and charging speeds are shown only where mappers have recorded them and are never guaranteed. Treat this finder as an excellent way to discover where stations are, then confirm the final details — plug type, price and live availability — in the operator's own app or on the station itself when you arrive.

    How It Works

    EV Charging Nearby is a small, honest tool built entirely on free, open services. This page explains exactly what happens when you run a search, where the data comes from, and what the results can and cannot promise.

    Step 1: Turning your search into a point on the map

    Every search starts with a single point — a latitude and longitude. If you tap "Use my location", your browser asks your device for its position and uses that. If you type a city, town or ZIP code instead, your browser sends the text to Nominatim, the free geocoding service run for the OpenStreetMap project, which converts the place name into coordinates. Nothing is typed into a server of ours: this website has no server at all, and your search runs entirely in your own browser.

    Step 2: Asking OpenStreetMap for stations

    With a point established, your browser queries the Overpass API, a read-only service that searches the OpenStreetMap database. The query asks for everything tagged as a charging station (in OpenStreetMap terms, amenity=charging_station) within the radius you chose — 2, 5, 10 or 25 miles. Because public Overpass servers can occasionally be busy, the finder asks three independent mirror servers at the same time and uses whichever answers first, with an overall limit of 60 seconds. If none of them can be reached, you will see an honest error message rather than a made-up result.

    Step 3: Measuring real distances

    OpenStreetMap returns the coordinates of each station. Your browser then calculates the straight-line distance from your search point to every station using the haversine formula — the standard way of measuring distance over the curved surface of the Earth — and sorts the list nearest first. These are "as the crow flies" distances in miles, so the drive itself will usually be a little longer; that is why every result carries a Directions link that opens the true road route in Google Maps. The embedded map on the results page is an OpenStreetMap view centred on your search point, so you can see the shape of the area at a glance.

    Where the data comes from — and its limits

    OpenStreetMap is a map of the world built by volunteers, a little like Wikipedia. Its charging station coverage is very good in most towns and cities and improves every week, but it is not perfect: a brand-new station may not be mapped yet, a removed one may linger, and details such as the number of sockets are recorded for some stations and not others. If you spot a station that is missing or wrong, you can create a free OpenStreetMap account and fix the map yourself — every driver who uses open data benefits.

    The most important limit to understand is availability. There is no free, open, live feed that reports whether an individual public charger is switched on, occupied or out of order at this moment. Commercial networks know the status of their own chargers, but they do not publish that information openly for tools like this one. So while we can tell you with confidence where stations are, we cannot guarantee that a particular charger will be free or working when you arrive, and connector types and charging speeds — where they appear in the data at all — are not guaranteed either. On any trip that matters, pick a backup station from the list before you set off, and confirm live details in the network operator's own app.

    Your privacy

    Because the whole tool runs in your browser, there is no account, no sign-up and no tracking by us. Your location is used only for the search you asked for. To answer that search, your coordinates or search text are necessarily sent to the OpenStreetMap services (Nominatim and the Overpass mirrors), which are operated by the OpenStreetMap community and its partners under their own privacy policies. You can read more in our privacy policy.

    EV Charging Levels Explained: Level 1, Level 2 and DC Fast Charging

    A plain-English guide to the three ways of charging an electric car.

    Ask how an electric car charges and you will quickly hear about "levels" — Level 1, Level 2 and DC fast charging. The labels come from North American charging standards, but the ideas behind them apply everywhere: they describe how much electrical power is flowing into the car, and therefore how quickly the battery fills up. Once you understand the three levels, the rest of EV ownership — home charging, public charging, road trips — falls into place. This guide explains each level in turn: how it works, how fast it is, where you will find it and what it is best used for.

    Level 1: the ordinary household socket

    Level 1 is the slowest form of charging, and the simplest: the car plugs into a standard household power outlet using the portable cable that comes with most electric cars. In North America that means a 120-volt supply; in much of the rest of the world, ordinary sockets deliver a similar gentle rate of charge. No installation, no special equipment, no electrician — if there is a socket within reach of the car, you can charge.

    The trade-off is speed. Level 1 typically adds around 3 to 5 miles of range for every hour the car is plugged in. That sounds painfully slow, and for a big battery it is: filling a large EV from empty could take two days or more. But speed is not everything. The average driver covers far fewer miles per day than they think, and a car parked at home for ten or twelve hours overnight gains 30 to 60 miles on Level 1 — enough to cover many people's daily driving entirely. Level 1 is also gentle on the battery and costs nothing to set up, which makes it a sensible starting point for drivers with modest daily mileage, plug-in hybrids with small batteries, and anyone keeping a second car topped up.

    Level 2: the everyday workhorse

    Level 2 charging uses a 240-volt AC supply — the same kind of circuit that powers an electric oven or a tumble dryer — delivered through a dedicated charging unit, often called a wallbox at home or a charging post in public. Most home chargers and the great majority of public chargers you will find with our station finder are Level 2.

    Level 2 is roughly three to seven times faster than Level 1. Depending on the car and the charger, it typically adds 20 to 40 miles of range per hour, which means a full charge overnight is realistic for almost any electric car, and a two-hour stop at a supermarket, gym or workplace adds a genuinely useful amount of range. This combination — fast enough for daily life, cheap enough to install widely — is why Level 2 has become the default: it is what most owners install at home, and what hotels, employers and car parks provide for guests and staff.

    Two details are worth knowing. First, the car's own onboard charger sets the ceiling: the car converts AC power from the post into DC power for the battery, and if the car can only accept 7 kW, a more powerful post will not charge it any faster. Second, public Level 2 charging is sometimes free — a point we explore in our guide to free EV charging — because businesses use it to attract customers who will stay for an hour or two.

    DC fast charging: built for the road

    DC fast charging — sometimes called rapid charging, or Level 3 in casual conversation, though that is not an official standard — works differently. Instead of sending AC power to the car and letting the car convert it, the charger itself does the conversion and feeds direct current straight into the battery, bypassing the car's onboard charger entirely. That is how it achieves far higher power: commonly 50 kW on older units, 150 kW on many modern ones, and 250 to 350 kW at the fastest sites.

    In practice, a DC fast charger can add 100 miles of range in roughly 15 to 30 minutes on a car that supports high charging speeds, and take a battery from 20 percent to 80 percent in about half an hour. This is the technology that makes long electric road trips practical: charge while you have a coffee and a break, then drive for another two or three hours. You will find DC fast chargers mainly along motorways and major routes, at dedicated charging hubs and at some fuel stations and retail parks.

    There are caveats. Fast charging is usually the most expensive way to charge per unit of energy. Not every car can use every charger — connector types differ (CCS and Tesla's NACS dominate in North America, CHAdeMO is an older standard still used by some cars), and older or smaller EVs may charge at modest DC speeds or not support DC charging at all. Charging also slows markedly as the battery fills, which is why experienced drivers unplug at around 80 percent on a trip — a point covered in detail in our article on how long it takes to charge an EV. Finally, live availability matters most at fast chargers, and as our how it works page explains honestly, no free data feed can guarantee a specific unit is free or working when you arrive, so plan a backup on important journeys.

    Which level do you actually need?

    For most drivers the answer is a mix. If you can charge where you park overnight, Level 1 may honestly be enough, and Level 2 at home is close to perfect — you wake up to a full battery every day and rarely think about charging at all. If you cannot charge at home, a dependable public Level 2 charger near home or work becomes your anchor. And for everyone, DC fast charging is the road-trip tool: not an everyday habit, but the thing that turns a 300-mile day into a coffee stop. Match the charger to the stop you were going to make anyway, and charging stops feeling like a chore at all.

    How Long Does It Take to Charge an Electric Car?

    Why charging time depends on the battery, the charger and one golden rule.

    "How long does it take to charge?" is the first question almost everyone asks about electric cars, and the honest answer — anywhere from half an hour to two days — is useless without context. Charging time is not a fixed property of a car. It is the result of three things: how much energy the battery needs, how fast the charger can supply it, and how fast the car can accept it. Understand those three, and you can predict the charging time of any car at any charger. This article explains each factor, then gives realistic time ranges for the chargers you will actually meet.

    The simple maths underneath

    Batteries are measured in kilowatt-hours (kWh) — the amount of energy they hold. Chargers are measured in kilowatts (kW) — the rate at which they deliver energy. Divide the energy you need by the rate, and you get hours. Adding 40 kWh of energy on a 10 kW charger takes about four hours; the same 40 kWh on a 150 kW fast charger takes, in theory, about sixteen minutes. Real life is a little slower than the theory, for reasons explained below, but this one calculation explains most of the differences you will see. If the levels themselves are new to you, our guide to charging levels explained is the place to start.

    Factor one: battery size and how empty it is

    A small city EV might carry a 40 kWh battery; a large family SUV might carry 100 kWh. Filling the big battery takes proportionally longer on the same charger. Just as important is how much energy you actually need to add. Drivers rarely charge from truly empty to truly full: a typical home session might add the 10 or 15 kWh used that day, and a typical rapid-charging stop adds enough to reach the next stop comfortably. Asking "how long to add the energy I need" gives a much friendlier answer than "how long from zero to one hundred percent."

    Factor two: the charger's power

    Chargers vary enormously. A Level 1 household socket delivers roughly 1.4 to 2 kW. A Level 2 home wallbox or public post usually delivers 7 to 11 kW, with some public units reaching 22 kW. DC fast chargers start around 50 kW and the newest reach 350 kW. On paper, then, the fastest chargers are more than a hundred times quicker than a household socket — which is why choosing the right charger for the stop matters more than anything else. Our station finder lists the public stations around you so you can pick the stop that fits your schedule.

    Factor three: the car itself, and the charging curve

    The car is the other half of the equation. On AC charging (Levels 1 and 2), the car's onboard charger sets the maximum rate, so a car limited to 7 kW gains nothing from a 22 kW post. On DC fast charging, the car's maximum DC rate is the ceiling — and even that headline figure is a peak, not a promise. Batteries charge fastest when they are fairly empty and slow down as they fill, to protect the battery's health; engineers call this the charging curve. A car advertised as charging at "up to 200 kW" might hold that speed only between 10 and 50 percent, then taper steadily. Cold weather slows things further, which is why many cars pre-condition the battery on the way to a fast charger when you set it as a navigation destination.

    The 20-to-80-percent rule

    These facts combine into the single most useful habit in EV driving: on a fast charger, arrive low and leave at about 80 percent. Between roughly 20 and 80 percent the battery charges at or near its best speed; the last 20 percent can take as long as everything before it. Two short stops between 20 and 80 percent are almost always quicker than one long stop to 100 percent. This is why manufacturers quote "10 to 80 percent" times — often 20 to 40 minutes on a modern car at a fast charger — and why experienced road-trippers plan around them.

    Realistic times, charger by charger

    Putting it together for a typical modern EV with a battery of around 60 kWh: on Level 1 at home, expect roughly 3 to 5 miles of range per hour — an overnight top-up, not a refill. On Level 2 at 7 to 11 kW, expect about 25 to 40 miles of range per hour, taking a near-empty battery to full in roughly six to nine hours — perfectly matched to a night's sleep or a working day. At a public Level 2 post during a two-hour shopping or cinema trip, expect to add a useful 50 to 80 miles. On a DC fast charger, expect 20 to 80 percent in around 25 to 45 minutes on most modern cars — faster on the newest models, slower on older ones — which is a coffee, a sandwich and a leg-stretch. And remember that many public Level 2 chargers cost little or nothing; our guide to free EV charging shows where to find them.

    Spending less time charging

    A few habits make charging time nearly disappear. Charge where the car is parked anyway — at home overnight, or at work — and you will almost never wait for charging at all. On trips, pair charging stops with breaks you would take regardless, and unplug at 80 percent. Keep the battery between about 20 and 80 percent in daily use rather than running it to empty, which keeps every top-up short. And before a journey, use the finder on this site alongside our how it works notes to line up a main stop and a backup, because the fastest charging plan is the one that does not depend on a single charger being free.

    How to Find Free EV Charging Near You

    Where free charging hides, how to spot it, and how to use it fairly.

    Public charging usually costs money, and rapid charging on a motorway can cost as much per mile as petrol. But scattered through every town is a quieter network of chargers that cost nothing at all — provided by businesses and organisations that see free electricity as a cheap way to win your custom, your booking or your loyalty. Free charging will rarely be the fastest option, and it comes with unwritten rules, but if you know where to look it can take a real bite out of your running costs. This guide explains where free charging hides, how to find it near you, and how to use it without spoiling it for everyone else.

    Why would anyone give charging away?

    The economics are simple. A Level 2 charger delivering power for two hours hands over roughly 14 kWh of electricity — a modest cost to the business — while the driver spends those two hours inside a shop, a restaurant, a hotel or a cinema spending considerably more. Free charging is a marketing expense, and a rather clever one: it attracts exactly the customers a business wants, keeps them on the premises longer, and costs less than most advertising. That is why the overwhelming majority of free chargers are Level 2 units suited to a dwell of an hour or more, rather than fast chargers for passing traffic — if the distinction is new to you, our charging levels explained guide covers it.

    Supermarkets and retail parks

    The classic home of free charging is the supermarket car park. Several large chains installed Level 2 posts and made them free for customers while they shop, sometimes activated with a tap of a bank card or the store's app even when the session itself is free. Retail parks, garden centres, furniture stores and outlet villages do the same. The catch is usually time: many car parks limit stays to two or three hours, and the charger is only free while you are a genuine customer. A weekly shop that takes an hour can quietly return 25 to 40 miles of range — which, repeated every week, adds up to serious free mileage over a year.

    Hotels, restaurants and attractions

    Hotels are the other great source. Destination chargers at hotels, guest houses and holiday parks are frequently free for overnight guests, and arriving with a low battery and leaving with a full one is one of the genuine pleasures of electric travel. Restaurants, pubs, visitor attractions and leisure centres increasingly offer the same for diners and ticket holders. The golden rule is to ask: some hotels switch chargers on at reception, some keep them for guests only, and a polite question at booking stage both reserves your charge and confirms it is actually free. If you are planning a trip, our article on how long it takes to charge an EV will help you judge whether an overnight Level 2 session covers the next day's driving — it almost always does.

    Workplaces, councils and car parks

    Many employers provide free workplace charging as a staff perk; if yours does not yet, it is worth asking, since incentives for installing workplace chargers exist in many regions. Local councils and public car parks are another source: some towns still offer free charging in municipal car parks to encourage EV adoption and town-centre visits, though parking charges may still apply even when the electricity does not. Universities, hospitals and park-and-ride sites round out the list. These chargers are often the least advertised, which is exactly why they are worth hunting for.

    How to actually find them

    Start with the finder on this site: search your town or the area you are visiting, and look at the stations at supermarkets, hotels and public car parks in the results — those categories are where free charging concentrates. Then verify before you rely on it. Here we have to be honest, as we are on our how it works page: map data records where chargers are, but pricing belongs to each operator, changes often, and is not reliably recorded in any free database — so no honest tool can simply label a station "free". Check the operator's app, the business's website, or reviews from recent visitors, and treat your first visit as a scouting trip. If a charger turns out to be paid, you have lost nothing; if it is free, you have found a regular stop worth keeping.

    The etiquette that keeps it free

    Free charging survives on goodwill, and a few simple habits protect it. Only plug in when you genuinely need charge, and move the car when you are done — a full car sitting on a free charger all day is the fastest way to get the charger removed or priced. Respect time limits and customer-only rules; they are the terms of the gift. Leave the cable tidy, report faults to the business rather than just driving off, and if the business is a café or shop, buying something is both fair and the entire point. Drivers who treat free chargers as shared community resources find they are still there next year; drivers who treat them as an entitlement tend to find a payment terminal fitted by spring.

    Is free charging worth the effort?

    For most drivers, free charging is a bonus rather than a strategy: home charging, if you have it, will still be your cheapest and most convenient option, and no one should drive ten miles out of their way to save the price of a coffee. But folded into journeys you are making anyway — the weekly shop, the overnight stay, the working day — free public charging quietly removes a noticeable slice of your running costs, and there is a particular satisfaction in a full battery that cost nothing. Find the stations first, verify the price second, and enjoy the miles.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    Straight answers about how the finder works, what its data can promise, and what it honestly cannot.

    How does EV Charging Nearby find charging stations?

    When you search, your browser asks the free OpenStreetMap services for charging stations recorded near your location. The place name is converted to coordinates by Nominatim, and the stations themselves come from the Overpass API, which reads the OpenStreetMap database. Distances are then calculated in your browser and the results are sorted nearest first. Our how it works page explains the process in full.

    Is the station information live? Can you guarantee a charger is free when I arrive?

    No. There is no free, open live feed that reports whether an individual public charger is working or occupied right now. Our results show stations that exist in the OpenStreetMap database, but a charger could be busy, out of service, or restricted when you arrive. Always have a backup station in mind on longer trips.

    Does the site show connector types and charging speeds?

    Only when OpenStreetMap volunteers have recorded them, and even then the details are not guaranteed. Connector types, socket counts and power ratings in community-mapped data can be incomplete or out of date, so check the charging network's own app or the signage at the station before you rely on a specific plug or speed. If you are unsure what the speeds mean, read our guide to charging levels explained.

    Is EV Charging Nearby free to use?

    Yes. The finder is completely free, needs no account and no sign-up. The underlying map data from OpenStreetMap is also free and open.

    Why did my search find no charging stations?

    The most common reasons are a small search radius, a rural area with few public chargers, or a place that OpenStreetMap volunteers have not fully mapped yet. Try widening the radius to 10 or 25 miles, or search for the nearest town or city instead of a small village.

    How accurate are the distances shown?

    Distances are straight-line distances calculated from the coordinates of your search point and each station, shown in miles. The actual driving distance will usually be a little longer because roads are not straight lines. Use the Directions link on a result to get the true road route in Google Maps.

    Does this website track or store my location?

    No. If you tap "Use my location", your position is read by your own browser and used only to run that one search. Your coordinates are sent to the OpenStreetMap services (Nominatim and Overpass) so they can answer the query, but this website has no server and stores nothing about you. See our privacy policy for details.

    Can I find free EV charging with this tool?

    The finder shows all mapped public stations, including ones at supermarkets, hotels, workplaces and public car parks where charging is sometimes free. Because pricing is set by each operator and changes often, the map data cannot reliably label a station as free, so our guide to finding free EV charging explains what to look for.

    What is the difference between Level 2 and DC fast charging?

    Level 2 charging uses a 240-volt AC supply and typically adds around 20 to 40 miles of range per hour, which suits home charging and longer stops. DC fast charging feeds direct current straight to the battery and can add 100 miles or more in about half an hour, which suits motorway and road-trip stops. Our charging levels guide explains all three levels in detail.

    Which charging networks and car brands does this work for?

    The finder is independent of any network or car brand. It lists stations from every operator that appears in OpenStreetMap — large networks, supermarket chargers, council car parks and small independent sites alike — so it works whatever electric car you drive.

    About EV Charging Nearby

    A free, independent tool that helps electric car drivers find public charging stations, built on open data.

    EV Charging Nearby exists for one simple reason: finding a public charger should not be the hardest part of owning an electric car. Drivers told the same story again and again — the car is wonderful, the charging is fine, but knowing where the nearest public station is, especially somewhere unfamiliar, meant juggling several apps, accounts and maps. We built a single page that answers that one question quickly: where are the charging stations near me, how far away are they, and how do I get to one?

    The site is deliberately built on open data. Station locations come from OpenStreetMap, the community-built map of the world that anyone can improve, and the searches run on the project's free Nominatim and Overpass services. That choice keeps the tool free for every visitor, independent of any single charging network, and honest about its limits — open data shows where stations are, but no free feed exists for live availability, a limitation we explain openly in our FAQ rather than papering over.

    Alongside the finder, we publish a small library of plain-English articles about charging in the real world: what the charging levels mean, how long charging actually takes, and how to find charging that costs nothing. Everything on the site is written by us, in our own words, for drivers rather than for engineers.

    EV Charging Nearby is not affiliated with any car manufacturer, charging network or energy company, and no operator can pay to appear higher in the results — the list is always simply nearest first. If you have a suggestion, a correction, or a station that should be on the map, get in touch.

    Privacy Policy

    Last updated: 2026.

    EV Charging Nearby is a static website with no server, no accounts and no database. That design shapes everything about your privacy: there is very little we could collect even if we wanted to, and we do not want to.

    What happens when you search

    All searching happens in your own web browser. If you use the "Use my location" button, your browser asks your device for its position; that position is used only to run the search you requested. Whether you search by location or by typing a place, your browser sends the search point or search text directly to the OpenStreetMap services that power the finder — Nominatim for place names and the Overpass API mirrors for station data — because those services need the location to answer the question. Those services are operated by the OpenStreetMap Foundation, its partners and community mirror operators, under their own privacy policies, and they may log requests (such as IP address and the query itself) for operational and abuse-prevention purposes, as most web services do.

    What we store

    Nothing. This website sets no accounts, keeps no search history, and does not store your location or searches anywhere. We do not run our own analytics on your visits and we do not build profiles of visitors. The page may keep a temporary note of your last search point in the browser's memory while the tab is open, purely so changing the search radius can re-run the same search; it disappears when you close the tab.

    Third-party services shown on the page
